Academic integrity

Academic integrity means the work you turn in honestly shows what you know and can do. AI doesn't change that principle. It just adds new ways to keep it or break it.

Schools handle AI very differently. One teacher may encourage AI brainstorming while another bans AI completely for a specific assignment. Many teachers describe AI use on a scale like this:

LevelWhat it meansExample
No AIThe work must be fully your own.In-class writing, most tests
AI for ideasAI can help you brainstorm or plan; the work is yours.Generating possible project topics
AI for feedbackAI can review your work; you do the revising.“Where is my argument weakest?”
AI as a collaboratorAI can help create parts of the work, with disclosure.Some design or coding projects

Three questions to ask yourself

  1. Is it allowed? Check the syllabus or ask. When it's unclear, ask before you use it.
  2. Am I still learning? If AI did the part the assignment is meant to teach, you skipped the learning.
  3. Would I be comfortable showing my teacher the chat? If not, that's a sign to rethink.
Heads up

AI-detection tools are not reliable enough to prove someone used AI, and they can wrongly flag human writing. That's another reason to be open about your process: keep your drafts and notes so you can show your work.

Citing & disclosing AI

When AI is allowed, you still need to be transparent about it. There are two common ways:

Disclosure
A short note explaining how you used AI, for example: “I used Claude to brainstorm three possible thesis statements. I chose and revised one, and all research and writing are my own.”
Citation
A formal reference, used when you quote or paraphrase AI-generated text. Both APA and MLA have published guidance on how to do this.

The formats

APA 7th edition treats the company as the author and the tool as the title:

OpenAI. (2023). ChatGPT (Mar 14 version) [Large language model]. https://chat.openai.com/chat

MLA 9th edition starts with a description of your prompt:

“Describe the symbolism of the green light in The Great Gatsby” prompt. ChatGPT, 13 Feb. version, OpenAI, 8 Mar. 2023, chat.openai.com/chat.

Examples follow the APA Style blog (McAdoo, 2023) and the MLA Style Center (2023). See our References.

Bias & fairness

In Module 1 you saw that one mislabeled example could bend a classifier's boundary. Now imagine the training data has millions of examples that reflect unfair patterns from the real world. The model learns those patterns too. This is called algorithmic bias.

A real example: Gender Shades

In 2018, researchers Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested commercial face-analysis systems from major tech companies. The systems were very accurate at classifying the gender of lighter-skinned men, with error rates under 1%. For darker-skinned women, error rates were as high as about 35%. A major reason: the data used to build and test these systems didn't fairly represent everyone.

Where bias comes from

  • Unrepresentative data: some groups appear much less often in the training data.
  • Historical patterns: data from the past can reflect past discrimination, like hiring records.
  • Design choices: what developers decide to measure and optimize for.
What you can do

When AI describes people, groups or cultures, ask: Whose perspective is missing? Is this a stereotype? Try asking the same question in different ways and compare the answers.

Privacy & safety

Anything you type into an AI tool leaves your device. Depending on the service and its settings, your conversations may be stored, reviewed, or used to train future models. A good rule: don't type anything into AI you wouldn't post publicly.

Never share

  • Passwords and login codes
  • Your full name with your address, phone or school
  • Other people's private messages or photos
  • Health, financial or other sensitive details

Safer to share

  • General questions about a topic
  • Your own writing, with personal details removed
  • Public information, like a published article
  • Made-up examples

Deepfakes and synthetic media

AI can now create realistic fake images, audio and video, called deepfakes. They can be used to spread false information, scam people by imitating a familiar voice, or harass someone. Creating fake sexual or humiliating images of a real person is harmful, and in many places illegal.

  • Be skeptical of shocking clips, especially ones that spread fast.
  • Check whether trusted news organizations are reporting the same thing (lateral reading again).
  • Never create or share fake media of real people, even as a joke.
  • If someone makes a deepfake of you or a friend, tell a trusted adult and report it to the platform.

The bigger picture

Responsible use goes beyond your own homework. AI raises questions that your generation will help answer:

Energy & environment
Training and running large models takes a lot of electricity and water for cooling data centers. The International Energy Agency estimated data centers used about 1.5% of the world's electricity in 2024, and expects that to grow as AI expands.
Work & careers
AI is changing many jobs: automating some tasks and creating new roles. Skills like critical thinking, communication and knowing how to work alongside AI are becoming more valuable.
Creativity & copyright
Many AI models were trained on artists' and writers' work, often without permission. Courts and lawmakers are still deciding how copyright applies.
Accountability
When an AI system makes a harmful decision, who is responsible? Frameworks like the U.S. NIST AI Risk Management Framework encourage organizations to build AI that is valid, safe, fair, transparent and accountable.
