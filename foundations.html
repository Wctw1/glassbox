What counts as AI?

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a broad name for computer systems that do tasks we usually connect with human thinking: recognizing images, understanding speech, making predictions, or writing text. You've probably used AI today without noticing. Your phone's face unlock, autocorrect, a music app's recommendations, and your email's spam filter all use it.

It helps to separate two very different ideas that both get called “AI”:

Narrow AI
A system built to do one kind of task, like translating languages or detecting tumors in scans. Every AI system that exists today is narrow AI, including chatbots, even though they can talk about many topics.
General AI (AGI)
A hypothetical system that could learn and reason across any task the way a person can. It's a research goal and a popular topic in movies, but it does not exist yet and experts disagree about when, or whether, it will.

Two ways to make a computer “smart”

Rules written by people

A programmer writes every rule: “If the email contains ‘FREE MONEY’, mark it as spam.” This is predictable and easy to explain, but it breaks the moment spammers write “FR3E M0NEY.”

Patterns learned from data

Instead of writing rules, we show the computer thousands of emails already labeled spam or not spam, and it figures out the patterns itself. This is machine learning, and it powers almost all modern AI.

Key idea

Modern AI is less about programming intelligence and more about learning patterns from huge amounts of data. That one fact explains most of AI's strengths, and most of its weaknesses.

Learning from examples

Machine learning (ML) is the process of a computer improving at a task by finding patterns in data. The collection of examples it learns from is called the training data. After training, the result is a model: a saved set of patterns that can make predictions about new data it has never seen.

Think of a spam filter. Each email can be described by features, which are measurable details like how many links it has, or how many words are in ALL CAPS. In training, each email also has a label: the correct answer, “spam” or “not spam.” The model's job is to learn which combinations of features usually go with which label.

Three main styles of learning

SupervisedFrom examples that come with correct answers (labels).Spam filters, recognizing handwritten digits

Neural networks

Our spam classifier only used two features. Recognizing a face or understanding a sentence takes thousands. For problems like that, AI researchers use neural networks: layers of simple math units loosely inspired by neurons in the brain.

How a network learns

  1. Guess. Data goes in the input layer, passes through the hidden layers, and the network produces an output. At first, it's mostly random.
  2. Measure the error. The output is compared to the correct label. The difference is the error, or loss.
  3. Adjust. Each weight is nudged a tiny bit in the direction that would have made the error smaller. This process is called gradient descent.
  4. Repeat millions of times over the whole training set, until the network is accurate on examples it has never seen.

When a network has many hidden layers, we call it deep learning. Today's largest models have billions of weights (often called parameters). No person sets those numbers by hand, which is why even the engineers who build a model can't always explain exactly why it gave a specific answer.

Try this

Next time an app recognizes your face or a song, remember: it isn't “seeing” like you do. It's running numbers through layers of weights that were tuned on millions of examples.

How chatbots write

Chatbots like ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot run on large language models (LLMs). An LLM is a very large neural network trained on an enormous amount of text, including books, websites and code. Its training goal sounds simple: predict the next piece of text.

LLMs don't read whole words the way you do. They break text into tokens, chunks that are often a word or part of a word. When you send a message, the model looks at all the tokens so far and calculates how likely every possible next token is. It picks one, adds it to the text, and repeats, one token at a time, until the answer is done.

From text predictor to helpful assistant

  1. Pre-training: the model learns language patterns by predicting the next token across a huge collection of text.
  2. Fine-tuning: it's trained further on example conversations so it follows instructions and answers questions.
  3. Human feedback: people rate responses, and the model is adjusted toward answers people found helpful and safe. This step is often called reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).

Where AI goes wrong

Knowing how AI works makes its failure points predictable. Here are the big ones every student should know:

Hallucination
The model produces information that sounds confident and specific but is false, like fake quotes, wrong dates, or citations for books and articles that don't exist. It happens because the model is generating likely-sounding text, not looking facts up.
Knowledge cutoff
A model only knows what was in its training data, which stops at a certain date. Unless a tool is connected to search, it may not know about recent events.
Bias
Models learn from human-created data, so they can repeat stereotypes and unfair patterns in that data. You'll explore this in Module 3.
No real understanding
An LLM can explain a math concept well and still get a simple calculation wrong. Fluent language is not proof of correct reasoning.
Sensitivity to wording
Small changes in how you ask can produce very different answers. That's why prompt writing (Module 2) matters.
Remember

Treat AI output like a first draft from a fast, well-read classmate who sometimes makes things up. Useful, but always worth checking.
