The AI toolbox

“AI” isn't one tool. Different tools are built for different jobs, and picking the right one saves time and cuts down on mistakes. Here are the main categories you're likely to run into. Product names change quickly, so focus on the type of tool.

Tool typeGood forWatch out forExamples
Chat assistantsExplaining concepts, brainstorming, feedback on draftsHallucinated facts and sourcesChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot
AI search / research toolsFinding sources, summarizing search results with linksSummaries that misstate what a source saysPerplexity, Google AI Overviews
Writing aidsGrammar, clarity, toneChanging your voice or meaningGrammarly, built-in editor tools
Tutoring toolsStep-by-step guidance without just giving answersStill needs checking on tricky problemsKhanmigo
Coding assistantsExplaining errors, suggesting codeCode that runs but has bugs or security holesGitHub Copilot
Image & media generatorsConcept art, mockups, visual ideasCopyright questions; realistic fakesAdobe Firefly, DALL·E
Check first

Many AI services set a minimum age (often 13) and some require a parent's permission for users under 18. Your school may also have approved tools with stronger privacy protections. Use what your school allows.

Writing better prompts

A prompt is what you type to an AI tool. Because the model predicts a likely response from your words, vague prompts get generic answers. Specific prompts get specific, useful ones. Strong prompts usually include five parts:

Task
Exactly what you want done. Use an action verb: explain, compare, quiz me, give feedback on.
Context
Who you are and why you're asking: your grade level, class, and what you already know.
Role
Who the AI should act as: a patient tutor, a tough debate coach, a museum guide.
Format
What the answer should look like: a table, 5 bullet points, under 150 words.
Limits
Rules to follow: don't give me the answer yet, use only the text I pasted, ask me one question at a time.

Weak prompt

explain the french revolution

You'll get a long, generic overview that may not match your class or what you need.

Strong prompt

I'm a 9th grader in World History. Act as a tutor. Explain the 3 main causes of the French Revolution in simple terms, one short paragraph each. Then ask me one question to check I understood.

Clear audience, task, format and a next step.

Prompting is a conversation. If the first answer isn't right, follow up: “Make it shorter,” “Give me an example from sports,” or “What's one thing you might have gotten wrong here?”

    Studying with AI

    The research on learning is clear on one thing: you learn by doing the thinking yourself by recalling, explaining and practicing. AI can either help with that or quietly replace it. The difference is how you use it.

    Replaces your thinking

    • “Write my essay.”
    • “Answer these homework questions.”
    • “Summarize the book so I don't have to read it.”

    Supports your thinking

    • “Quiz me on my notes.”
    • “Explain where my reasoning went wrong.”
    • “Give me feedback, but don't rewrite it.”

    Five study prompts worth saving

    Copy these, then replace the brackets with your topic. Always check your teacher's AI policy first.

    Explain [topic] like I'm new to it. Use one real-world example, then check my understanding with one question.
    Here are my class notes: [paste notes]. Make 10 practice questions from them. Wait for my answers before telling me if I'm right.
    Act as a Socratic tutor for [topic]. Don't give me answers. Ask me guiding questions until I figure it out.
    Here is my draft: [paste draft]. Point out the 3 weakest parts of my argument. Do not rewrite anything.
    My [subject] test is in [number] days. I struggle with [topics]. Make a short daily study plan using active recall.
    Study tip

    After an AI explains something, close the tab and try to explain it back in your own words, out loud or on paper. If you can't, you haven't learned it yet.

    Check everything

    Since AI can hallucinate, the most valuable AI skill isn't prompting. It's verifying. Professional fact-checkers use a habit called lateral reading: instead of staring at one source to decide if it's trustworthy, they open new tabs and see what other reliable sources say.

    A simple way to remember the process is SIFT, a method developed by digital-literacy researcher Mike Caulfield:

    S: Stop
    Before you believe or use a claim, pause. Ask: do I know this source? Does this seem too perfect or too shocking?
    I: Investigate
    Investigate the source. Who made it, and are they in a position to know?
    F: Find
    Find better coverage. Search for the claim and see whether trusted sources agree.
    T: Trace
    Trace claims, quotes and data back to the original. Does the original actually say that?

    Red flags in AI answers

    • Very specific numbers, dates or quotes with no source.
    • Citations you can't find in a library database or search engine.
    • Different answers when you ask the same question again.
    • Claims about recent events (the model may be past its knowledge cutoff).
